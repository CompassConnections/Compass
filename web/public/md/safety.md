# Staying safe on Compass

Compass is an open directory, which is its whole point: you decide who is worth meeting instead of an
algorithm deciding for you. The trade-off is that openness works both ways — a small number of people here are not
who they say they are.

That openness also works in your favour, and it is the main reason there are far fewer fakes here than
on a typical swiping app. Every profile is visible to every member, so an account pretending to be
someone it is not is exposed to the whole community at once instead of being shown privately to one
target at a time. People notice, and they report — usually within days.

This page is the pattern-recognition guide we would want a friend to have:
what a scam actually looks like from the inside, how to check that someone is real, and how to make a
first meeting safe without making it joyless. Read it once and you will recognise most of it on sight.

If something here does not match what the app does, that is a bug — [tell us](/contact) and we will fix
the page or the code.

## The short version

If you remember nothing else:

- **Stay on Compass** until you trust the person. Someone who insists on moving to WhatsApp or Telegram
  in the first few messages is asking you to step somewhere no one is watching / reporting.
- **Get on a live video call** before you invest real feeling, and certainly before you meet. You can do
  that without giving anyone your number — see
  [calling without handing over your number](/safety#the-reluctance-to-video-call).
- **Never send money**, crypto, gift cards or bank details to someone you have not met — no matter how
  good the story is, and no matter how long you have been talking.
- **Meet in public**, get yourself there and back.
- **Tell a friend** where you are going, who with, and when you expect to be home.
- **Don't share** your home address, your workplace, your financial details or intimate images with
  someone you have only met online.
- **Report and block** anything that feels off. You do not owe anyone a polite exit.

None of this is about being suspicious of everyone. It is about making the small number of bad actors
run out of road early, so the rest of your conversations can be open ones.

## The off-platform push

This is the single most reliable early warning sign, and it comes before all the others.

A few messages in, they want to move to WhatsApp, Telegram, Signal, Instagram or email. The reason will
sound ordinary: _"I barely check this app"_, _"it's easier on WhatsApp"_, _"my account here keeps
glitching"_.

Why it matters: on Compass, an account that behaves badly can be reported, reviewed and removed, and
the pattern is visible to our moderators across everyone that account has contacted. Off-platform, none
of that exists. A scammer's first job is always to get you somewhere they cannot be reported.

**What to do.** There is nothing wrong with swapping numbers eventually — most connections that go
anywhere do. The rule is _sequence_: video call first, trust first, then the number. If someone cannot
wait a few days inside the app to keep talking to you, that impatience is the information.

**Be especially careful if** the push to move comes in the same breath as a compliment, a hard-luck
story, or an excuse about their account being deleted soon.

## The reluctance to video call

A real person who wants to meet you will get on camera. It is the cheapest, fastest test there is.

Watch for the escalating excuses: the camera is broken, the connection is bad, they are shy, they are
offshore / deployed / on a rig with no bandwidth, they will do it _next_ week. Watch for a call that
connects and then is all audio, or thirty frozen seconds of a face followed by "it keeps cutting out".
Recorded video sent as a "live" clip counts as a refusal, not a call.

**What to do.** Suggest a short call early and casually — ten minutes, no big deal. Ask them to wave, or
to hold up a hand, or to say your username; a pre-recorded clip or a manipulated stream cannot follow an
unexpected instruction. If the second attempt is another excuse, stop investing. You are not being harsh;
you are declining to keep paying attention to someone who will not show you a face.

### Calling without handing over your number

Compass has no calling built in yet, and every obvious workaround — WhatsApp, Telegram, Signal, FaceTime
— costs you a phone number or an email address, which is the one thing this page keeps telling you not to
give away early. You do not have to. Use a browser room link instead: one of you creates it, pastes it
into the Compass chat, and you both join in a tab. Nothing about you travels with it.

- **[Jitsi Meet](https://meet.jit.si)** — free, no account on either side, runs in the browser. Start a
  meeting, give the room an unguessable name, paste the link into the conversation here.
- **[Whereby](https://whereby.com)** — free tier, also browser-based. Whoever creates the room needs an
  account; whoever joins does not, so offer to be the one who makes it.
- **Google Meet or Zoom** — fine if you already use them, and joining does not show the other person your
  email. Create a fresh one-off meeting rather than sending your personal room link, which stays open
  forever.

Four habits that make this cost you nothing:

- **Set your display name to a first name before you join.** All of these default to whatever your
  account is called, which for a work Google account is often your full name and employer.
- **Make the link single-use.** A guessable room name, or a personal meeting room you reuse, can be
  walked into later by anyone who has ever held the link.
- **Look at what is behind you** before the camera goes on — a window with a recognisable view, post on
  the table, a parcel with your address. A blur or virtual background is one click in every tool above.
- **Never install anything to take a call.** "Download this app and I'll call you on it" is not a video
  call; anyone steering you to AnyDesk, TeamViewer or a "secure caller" wants your device, not your face.
  For the same reason, a call link that demands your Google or Facebook password is a phishing page —
  Jitsi and Whereby let you join as a guest. Close the tab and report the account.

And if a no-account browser link is met with _"I can only do video on WhatsApp"_, that is the
off-platform push again, wearing a different hat.

**Note.** Deepfaked video calls exist, and they are getting better. They are still poor at spontaneity —
odd hands, faces that break when someone turns fully sideways, audio that leads or lags the mouth. A
video call is strong evidence, not proof. It is meeting in person that settles it.

## Money, in every disguise

**No genuine connection made online will ever need your money.** Nothing in this section has an
exception, and the ask never arrives first — it arrives after weeks of warmth, and it is designed to feel
like helping someone you already care about.

The shapes it takes:

- **The emergency.** A medical bill, a stranded relative, a customs fee to release a package, a phone
  cut off. Urgent, one-off, and always just out of reach of their own accounts.
- **The investment tip.** They are doing well in crypto or forex and want to show you how. There is a
  platform, an app, a "mentor". Your first small withdrawal even works — that is what makes you trust it
  with more. This one is patient and can run for months; it is the most costly scam on any meeting
  platform, and its victims are usually financially literate people.
- **The travel plan.** They will finally come and see you, but the flight, the visa, or the border fee
  needs covering. Once the first one is paid there is always another.
- **The favour.** Receive this transfer and forward it on. Let a package come to your address. Accept a
  cheque and send part of it back. This is money laundering, and the criminal liability lands on you.
- **The reversal.** They send _you_ money unprompted, then need some of it back. The original payment is
  stolen or will be clawed back; the part you send is real.

**What to do.** Say no, once, plainly, and expect it not to be accepted the first time — the pressure that
follows tells you everything. Never send money, crypto, gift cards, prepaid vouchers, bank credentials or
card details to someone you have not met in person. Do not accept money either. Then
[report the account](/safety#reporting-and-blocking): the person who asked you is asking twenty other people, and
the report is what stops the twenty-first.

If you have already sent something, go straight to [if something has gone wrong](/safety#if-something-has-gone-wrong).
It is worth acting even hours later.

## Too much, too soon

Scams run on a schedule, because attention costs the scammer money.

Inside a week you are "the one", "soulmate", "the only person who has ever understood them". There are
good-morning and good-night messages every day, long declarations, plans for a shared life — all with
someone who has never seen your face move. This is called love bombing, and it works because it is
genuinely pleasant. Its purpose is to build enough emotional debt that the later ask feels like a small
thing between people who love each other.

The related pattern is the **borrowed life story**: a profile that maps suspiciously well onto yours, an
occupation that explains why they cannot video call or meet (offshore engineer, surgeon abroad, soldier
deployed, contractor in a country you have never been to), and a recent tragedy — a dead spouse, a child
in care — that arrives early and invites you to be the one who helps.

**What to do.** Slow the pace down on purpose and see what happens. Genuine intensity survives a slow week.
Manufactured intensity gets frustrated, then guilt-trips you, then leaves.

## Checking that someone is who they say they are

You can do most of this in ten minutes, and it costs the honest person nothing.

- **Reverse image search their photos.** Google Lens, TinEye or Yandex. A stock photo, a model's portfolio,
  or the same face under three names is the end of the conversation.
- **Look for a life, not a profile.** Real people accumulate untidy traces: a workplace, a club, an old
  account, friends who tag them. A person with one flawless set of photos and no other trace anywhere is
  worth a second look.
- **Ask something unrehearsed.** Not "where do you live" — what the walk from their place to the nearest
  station is like, what was busy in their town last weekend, what the food is at the place they mentioned.
  Scripts and translation tools handle biography well and specifics badly.
- **Watch the language change.** Fluent messages that suddenly turn stilted, a timezone that does not match
  the story, or a name that is spelled differently in two places usually means more than one person is
  typing.
- **Check the compatibility answers.** On Compass, answers are explicit and inspectable. A profile built
  purely of flattery, with nothing specific enough to disagree with, is doing bait, not honesty.

Being wrong about someone is not a failure of intelligence. These are professionalised operations with
scripts, shift patterns and training material. The only real defence is the checklist, not instinct.

## Photos, intimate images and sextortion

Intimate images sent to someone you have not met can be used against you, and increasingly the request for
them is the entire point of the account. The pattern is quick: rapid flirtation, a nude sent to you first
to make it feel mutual, and within minutes of yours arriving, a demand for money and a list of your
contacts they claim to have. It moves faster than any other scam here — often the same evening.

**What to do.**

- Treat any image you send as permanent and copyable, whatever the app promises. Faces and identifiable
  backgrounds are what make an image usable against you.
- If it has already happened: **do not pay**. Paying marks you as someone who pays, and the demands
  continue. Stop replying, take screenshots of everything including the account and payment details,
  block them, and report the account here and on the platform where it happened.
- You can have the images taken down without them being public. [StopNCII.org](https://stopncii.org) does
  this for adults using image hashes, so your photos never leave your device; if you are under 18, use
  [Take It Down](https://takeitdown.ncmec.org). Both are free.
- Tell someone you trust. Isolation is the leverage — it stops working the moment you have told a friend.

This happens to careful people, and it is a crime committed against you. There is nothing here to be
ashamed of.

## Meeting in person

Meeting is the goal. The point of everything above is to make sure it is a good one.

**Before:**

- **Video call first.** Not optional. Everything looks different once you have seen someone talk.
- **Pick the place yourself**, and make it a public one with staff and other people in it — a café, a bar
  before it empties, a busy park, a gallery. Not their home, not yours, not a place only they know, not a
  first meeting in another city.
- **Arrange your own transport, both ways.** No lift, no being picked up, no ride you cannot end. Have the
  fare home available separately from the evening.
- **Keep the first one short and daytime if you can.** Coffee that can end in forty minutes is far easier
  to leave than dinner and a booking.
- **Do not have them collect you from home**, and do not give your address until the address stops
  mattering.

**During:**

- **Keep your drink with you**, and take a fresh one if you leave it. Drink spiking is rare and real, and
  the whole defence is that simple. If you feel drunk far beyond what you have had, tell a member of staff
  immediately and ask them to help you leave — every venue takes this seriously.
- **Keep your phone charged and your own money on you.**
- **Leave when you want to leave.** You do not need a reason, and "I'm going to head off" is a complete
  sentence. Anyone worth meeting again will be fine with it.
- **Trust the discomfort.** Pressure to go somewhere private, to drink more, to move to a car, or anger at
  a small no — that is the whole warning. Awkwardness is a cheap price.

**After:** message your friend that you are home. If it went well, that is a nice message to send. If it
did not, they already know where you were.

## Tell a friend

The habit that does the most work for the least effort: before you meet someone new, tell one person the
plan.

Send them the name and profile link, the place and time, and when you expect to be back. Share your live
location for the evening — every phone does this natively. Agree a check-in message, and agree what they
should do if it does not arrive. If you would rather not explain, "I'm meeting someone new, here's where"
is enough; nobody needs the details.

If you have no one to send it to today, tell us — you can write to the team from the
[contact form](/contact), and a member of the community team can be the person expecting your check-in.

## Meeting at Compass events

Events are the part of Compass most likely to put you in a room with strangers, so the same rules apply
with one addition: an event is a public commitment, not a private invitation. Go to the event itself,
arrive and leave under your own steam, and treat an invitation to somewhere else afterwards exactly like a
first meeting — because it is one.

Hosts are members, not staff. A host asking for payment outside the platform, insisting on a private venue
address before you commit, or pressing you to attend alone, is worth reporting to us before you go.

## Reporting and blocking

Reporting is not an escalation and it is not a public act — the person never learns you did it. It is the
only way we see a pattern that spans many members, so it is genuinely the most useful thing you can do for
everyone else here.

**To report or block someone:** open their profile, use the **⋯** menu at the top, and choose **Report** or
**Block**. Blocking ends the conversation and hides you from each other. Inside a conversation, the same
options are in the **⋮** menu in the message header.

**Report if you see:** requests for money in any form, a profile using someone else's photos, an immediate
push to another app, sexual pressure or unwanted explicit images, threats or blackmail, anyone who appears
to be under 18, or an account that seems to be one of many.

**Please include** what happened, roughly when, and any usernames or links — the more specific the report,
the faster it can be acted on. If it is urgent or complex, write to us directly through the
[contact form](/contact).

We review reports and remove accounts that break the [terms](/terms). Repeat and severe cases are banned
outright, and we act on the pattern across accounts, not only on the single message you saw.

## If something has gone wrong

Act quickly, and skip the part where you feel stupid — these operations are professional, and their
targets are ordinary people paying ordinary attention.

1. **Stop contact.** Do not warn them, do not argue, do not try to recover the money by paying more. Cut it
   there.
2. **Keep the evidence** before you block: screenshots of the profile and the conversation, usernames,
   phone numbers, wallet addresses, bank details, and the platform it moved to. This is what makes a bank
   or police report actionable.
3. **Call your bank immediately** if money moved — same day matters, and transfers can sometimes be recalled.
   Ask for their fraud team. If you gave out card or account details, treat them as compromised. If you
   installed anything they asked you to install, or shared a screen, treat that device's passwords as
   compromised too.
4. **Report it to the police or your national fraud body.** In the
   UK, [Action Fraud](https://www.actionfraud.police.uk);
   in the US, the [FTC](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov) and the [FBI's IC3](https://www.ic3.gov); in the EU,
   your national police cybercrime unit. Romance fraud is a crime, and reports are what fund the units that
   pursue it.
5. **Report the account to us** so they cannot do it to the next person, and block them.
6. **Tell someone.** The single strongest predictor of how badly this goes is whether the person kept it to
   themselves.

**If you are in immediate danger, call your local emergency number** — 112 across the EU and UK, 911 in the
US and Canada — before anything on this page.

## What Compass does on its side

We would rather tell you what we actually do than imply more:

- We review every report, and we remove accounts for scam behaviour, impersonation, harassment and
  underage use — including on patterns we detect ourselves, not only on reports.
- Blocked members cannot message you or see you, and a removed account's conversations show a warning.
- We do not run background checks or identity verification, and we do not vet members before they join.
  An open directory cannot promise you that everyone in it is safe; it can promise you the tools and the
  information to decide for yourself, which is what this page is.
- Your profile visibility is yours to set in the profile [settings](/profile) — members-only is one switch away, and
  it hides you from logged-out visitors and search engines entirely.
- How we handle your data is in the [privacy policy](/privacy); the rules everyone agrees to are in the
  [terms](/terms); vulnerabilities in the platform itself go to [security](/security).

Anything else — questions, doubts, an account you are unsure about, or a first meeting you would like a
second opinion on — reach us through the [contact form](/contact) or the [help page](/help). We would much
rather hear from you early.
