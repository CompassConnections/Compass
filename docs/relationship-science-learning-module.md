# Finding and keeping someone: what the research actually says

A learning module for members, drawn from Paul Eastwick's _Bonded by Evolution_ (Crown, 2026) and the
research it reviews. The full text and a summary are in
[`martin/resources/bonded_by_evolution.md`](../martin/resources/bonded_by_evolution.md); the product-side
companion is [`relationship-science-platform-ideas.md`](relationship-science-platform-ideas.md).

**Status: draft content, not yet in the app.**

---

## How to use this

Eleven modules. Each one is built the same way:

> **What you probably believe** → **What the research shows** → **What to do differently** → **This week**

They are ordered deliberately: modules 1–4 clear out beliefs that make people behave badly, 5–8 are about
searching, 9–11 are about what to do once you've found someone. You can read them out of order, but 5
("choose your pool") only makes sense after 4 ("compatibility is built"), because it's the practical
consequence of it.

Two ground rules before you start.

**Effect sizes, not verdicts.** Where a finding has a number, it's given as a guessing game. "Small" means
that if you had to guess which of two people scores higher, you'd be right ~55% of the time. "Medium" ≈ 65%.
"Large" ≈ 75%. Anything near 50% is a coin flip, and a lot of famous relationship advice turns out to sit
there.

**This is not a system.** The single most consistent finding in the whole field is that who clicks with whom
is not predictable in advance — not by you, not by a questionnaire, not by a machine-learning model with a
hundred variables about both people. What follows isn't a method for identifying the right person. It's a
set of corrections to habits that make finding _anyone_ harder than it needs to be.

## Module 1 — You do not have a number

**What you probably believe.** That you have a rough desirability level — a 6, a 7 — set by your looks, your
status, your charisma; that people mostly agree on it; and that rejection tells you what it is.

**What the research shows.**

Any judgment one person makes about another breaks into three parts: **consensus** (agreement about the
person being judged — this is what "desirability" means), **selectivity** (how generous the judge is in
general), and **compatibility** (the unique fit between those two people). "Mate value" only exists to the
extent consensus is large. Across 17 studies:

| Context                                 | How much is consensus?                          |
| --------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- |
| Photographs of strangers                | Medium-to-large — but still **under half**      |
| First face-to-face meeting              | About the same                                  |
| After a few weeks of knowing each other | Small                                           |
| Among people who actually know you      | **Below "small" — 53% agreement.** Near chance. |

The part that grows as consensus shrinks is **compatibility**. Some supporting numbers:

- Across ~200 photographs rated by ~40 people each, all raters agreed on merely "top half" or "bottom half"
  for **4%** of faces. For 96% of faces, someone put it in their top half while someone else put the same
  face in their bottom half.
- In a study tracking **over 1,000 crushes** across months, how attractive the crush was rated by neutral
  coders predicted romantic interest at **essentially zero**. Not a liability — irrelevant.
- **96%** of speed-daters in one study received at least one second-date offer.
- Couples do match on attractiveness (you'd guess right ~70% of the time) — but that effect is large only
  for couples who got together _fast_, and much weaker for couples who were friends first, which is
  **two-thirds of couples**.
- "Date in your league or you'll get dumped" has no support. A meta-analysis found mismatched couples break
  up no sooner than matched ones.

**What to do differently.**

Stop treating rejection as information about your rank. It is mostly information about one person's
idiosyncratic taste, and the longer someone has known you, the more true that is. The corollary is more
useful than the reassurance: **your attractiveness advantage or disadvantage is at its maximum in photos and
first meetings, and fades from there.** If you don't photograph well or don't sparkle in the first ten
minutes, contexts that involve repeated exposure are not a consolation prize — they are the specific
environment where the thing you're bad at stops counting.

**This week.** When a rejection lands, say the replacement thought out loud. The one the research on
loneliness actually tests:

> _"Even if they reject me, attraction is mostly idiosyncratic, and it has absolutely no bearing on my value
> as a long-term partner."_

You have to catch the self-protective thought first ("she'll obviously say no"), which is the hard part. It
gets easier with practice; this is standard cognitive-behavioural work and it's the best-evidenced treatment
for loneliness there is.

## Module 2 — Men and women want nearly the same things

**What you probably believe.** That women prioritise status and earning potential and men prioritise looks;
that men want commitment less; that ambition is a liability for a woman and vulnerability is a liability for
a man.

**What the research shows.**

All of that comes from surveys — from what people _say_. It replicates reliably across dozens of countries.
It also stops being true the moment you measure what people actually respond to.

- At speed-dating events, the revealed preference for **earning potential** was identical for men and women
  (modest for both). The revealed preference for **attractiveness** was identical for men and women (about
  twice as strong as earning potential, for both). Women in that study were **twice as likely as men to say**
  they cared about earning potential — and it made no difference to who they actually liked.
- A meta-analysis of ~100 studies across 15 countries, then a 2025 study of **>10,000 people across 60 labs
  and 43 countries**: in real relationships, having an ambitious partner predicts satisfaction (medium) and
  having an attractive partner predicts satisfaction (large) — **identically for men and women**.
- The "she trades looks for his money" pattern disappears once you account for attractive people marrying
  attractive people, rich people marrying rich people, and wealth and attractiveness being correlated. Men
  and women are **equally likely** to use looks to attract a well-off partner.
- Men earning slightly more than their partners is fully explained by "everyone would prefer a partner who
  earns more" plus the pay gap. In newly married couples with different education levels, **the wife is now
  generally the more educated one**.
- There is **not a shred of evidence** that men want commitment less. Men and women don't differ in their
  stated desire for a long-term relationship, and once in one they're equally committed.
- A machine-learning study of **>11,000 couples** found gender to be one of the _worst_ predictors of
  relationship satisfaction. Age, self-esteem, neuroticism and family history all mattered more.

**The real differences, stated accurately.** Men are more interested in casual sex — medium-to-large, robust
worldwide. Three things drive it: (1) a lower threshold — the attraction level needed to want to _kiss_
someone is ~60/100 for both genders, but the level needed to want _sex_ stays at 60 for men and rises to 80
for women; (2) women's well-founded expectation that male casual partners are worse lovers (bisexual women
strongly prefer women for casual sex, and the orgasm gap explains why); (3) **stranger danger** — in the
classic field study, **38% of men and 2% of women** said yes to a proposition from an attractive stranger, but
**40% of women** said yes when the proposer was someone they already knew. Within relationships the desire
gap is small (57% guessing accuracy), and the bigger effect is that **men underestimate their partner's
desire about half the time** — twice as often as they overestimate it.

**What to do differently.**

- **Women:** stop downplaying ambition, income or education. Men respond to ambition exactly as much as women
  do; they just don't _think_ they do. Also: your age filter is probably costing you more than you think —
  in age-stratified speed-dating events, younger partners did better for both genders equally, and women's
  filters systematically exclude the younger men who'd be interested.
- **Men:** the stoic-provider act is unnecessary and it costs you. Being seen as warm, responsive and
  trustworthy has _no_ downside in the short term and is what predicts satisfaction in the long term. And if
  you're in a relationship: you are probably wrong about how much your partner wants you. Check rather than
  assume.
- **Everyone:** the reason you _believe_ in the gendered preferences is a confusion between "what the other
  gender is like on average" (men do earn more; women do invest more in appearance) and "what I want in a
  partner." Those are different questions.

**This week.** Look at your own filters. Which of them encode a belief about what the other gender wants,
rather than what you want? Widen one of them.

## Module 3 — Nobody is "just a hookup" or "marriage material"

**What you probably believe.** That some people are players and some are partner material, that you can spot
the difference, and that being too available or having a history reduces your chances of something serious.

**What the research shows.**

The whole cads-vs-dads model makes three predictions, and all three fail:

1. **Attractive, confident, sexually open people avoid relationships?** The opposite. They have _more_
   long-term relationships in their history — they catch feelings for hookup partners like everyone else. If
   you know someone is single, their attitudes to casual sex have **precisely zero** ability to predict
   whether they'll be partnered a year later.
2. **They make worse partners?** No. The effect of someone's rated attractiveness on their partner's
   satisfaction is about **zero**. Same for sexual openness. Social confidence is, if anything, slightly
   _positive_. And attractive people are **no more likely to cheat** — infidelity is predicted by the state
   of the relationship, plus problem drinking and opportunity.
3. **Kind, dependable people do worse at casual dating?** No. Being seen as friendly, responsive and
   trustworthy has **no bearing** on how many partners someone has had.

Two more findings that dissolve the categories entirely:

- When people rate someone they've just met on short-term and long-term appeal, the two judgments coincide
  **86%** of the time. Only 14% of people are meaningfully high on one and low on the other. On a first date,
  when he's rude to the waiter, you're not weighing _boyfriend vs hookup_ — you're just thinking _no_.
- **Where you meet has no bearing on the outcome.** "eharmony is responsible for a lot of casual sex, and
  Tinder is responsible for a lot of marriages."

What actually happens instead: romantic interest follows an **arc**. There's an early phase where attraction
and passion dominate, and consensually attractive, charismatic people do have a real advantage. Then there's
an attaching phase where an emotional bond either forms or doesn't, and where nobody has a predictable
advantage. When people chart their real relationships, short-term and long-term trajectories **diverge at
about the point things become sexual** — which inverts the usual logic. Casual relationships aren't _for_
sex; **good sex is what propels a casual thing into a serious one**. A "short-term relationship" is mostly a
euphemism for _I was a little into you_.

**What to do differently.**

Stop screening for players. It's the single biggest waste of energy in modern dating, you can't do it, and
the trait you'd be screening on doesn't predict what you think it predicts. Replace the sorting question
with **"let's see where this goes."** The two failure modes to watch for in the uncertain early phase are
opposite and equally costly: overconfidence (_I can just tell we're meant to be_) and pre-emptive bailing
(_any hint he's a player and I'm out_).

**This week.** Identify one person you wrote off on a category judgment — too hot, too popular, too casual,
too much history, seemed too interested, seemed not interested enough. Notice that you have no evidence.

## Module 4 — Compatibility is built, not matched

**What you probably believe.** That somewhere out there is someone whose personality, values and interests
fit yours, and that the job is to find them.

**What the research shows.** Every version of that idea has now been tested and failed.

- **Similarity.** Real couples are similar on nearly everything measurable — but that's _proximity_, not
  preference. People still marry within their own zip code about two-thirds of the time. In one study of the
  dating histories of **>500 young adults**, exes looked strikingly similar (e.g. similarly religious) —
  until you accounted for the local pool, at which point **all** of it turned out to be location: the people
  they _didn't_ date nearby were equally religious. In every speed-dating study ever run, similarity fails to
  predict attraction. Across dozens of studies, no form of similarity — traits, demographics, interests,
  attachment styles, even politics — reliably predicts satisfaction.
- **Your ideal-partner criteria.** In a pre-registered study of **>10,000 people in 43 countries** rating 35
  traits, matching predicts attraction — modestly, and **only in aggregate across all 35**. Any single trait
  contributes nothing. And the killer: **your three must-haves work no better than a stranger's three
  must-haves.** Ceiling estimate for all partner preferences combined: **3–4%** of compatibility.
- **Love languages.** The five behaviours are lovely (quality time and words of affirmation help most). But
  the _matching_ claim — the entire selling point — has **no meaningful effect** on satisfaction when
  properly measured.
- **Opposites attract.** No evidence, for any trait. Not even for masculine/feminine complementarity —
  relationships do better when **both** partners are warmer and more communal.
- **Algorithms.** With modern machine learning and rich pre-meeting data on both people, predicting which
  speed-dating pairs actually clicked achieved **zero percent** success. In established couples, the
  combination of two people's traits explained **at most 2%** of compatibility. Popularity is predictable;
  compatibility is not.

The causal arrow you've assumed runs backwards. It isn't that you notice similarities and then feel
attracted. **You feel attracted, and then go on a similarity hunt** — he likes climbing, you've been meaning
to try that; she's a foodie, you're basically a foodie-in-training. Then motivated reasoning inflates the
agreement and minimises the genuine differences.

So what _is_ compatibility? A history. Human courtship is a long, revocable, non-linear sequence of
interactions with no single moment of choosing. What makes our species distinctive is **re-mating
preferences**: _that was good, let's do that again_. Two people who keep doing that build a **tiny
two-person culture** — private language, nicknames, rituals, a story about who they are — and _that_ is what
compatibility means. It's also why the reasons you're compatible with one person don't transfer to the next.

One more thing worth knowing: how a conversation goes is genuinely chaotic. Three equally reasonable replies
to the same remark can lead to two dead ends and one spark, and there's no way to know in advance which.
"Most pairs do have something to bond over — it just takes a little (or a lot of) luck to find it."

**What to do differently.**

Drop your dealbreaker list to the things that are genuinely _structural_ — where you'd have to live, whether
you want children, whether you want monogamy, whether you want a partner who treats your career as equal to
theirs. Those are real trade-offs. Everything else — personality type, hobbies, diet, taste — is not
predictive and is costing you options. Then stop asking _"is this person right for me?"_ and start asking
**"do they have two or three qualities I'd want to build a relationship around?"** Green flags, not red ones.

**This week.** Write down your three must-haves. Now write down someone else's three must-haves — a friend's,
or just three different plausible ones. Sit with the finding that a partner meeting the second set would make
you about as happy as one meeting yours.

## Module 5 — Choose your pool, not your picks

This is the practical consequence of module 4, and it's the most important module here.

**What the research shows.** If compatibility can't be predicted but does tend to emerge given time and
repeated contact, then screening harder within a pool buys you almost nothing — and changing _which pool_
buys you nearly everything. Eastwick states it flatly: _"the decision about who fits within your field of
eligibles is your primary avenue for exerting control over how your romantic life will unfold."_ And, given
enough people whose life goals actually align with yours: _"I can virtually guarantee that you will end up
discovering and creating compatibility with one of them."_

Which means: if you keep dating the same disappointing person over and over, that is **usually a structural
problem, not a taste problem.** It's about where and how you're meeting people, not about your discernment
within the pool. The fix is a change of scenery, not stricter standards.

Supporting evidence for how much sheer exposure does: in a classroom with randomly assigned seating, students
seated next to each other liked each other **nearly 20× more on day one** and were more likely to be friends
by the end of the year. Randomly assigned repeated conversations increase liking each time. Familiarity
breeds liking, not contempt.

**What to do differently.**

Three concrete moves, in order of leverage:

1. **Filter on the structural, not the aesthetic.** If you're certain you don't want children, don't spend
   time around people who are certain they do — that trade-off is real and filtering is the right tool.
   If you want a partner who treats your career as equal, don't build a social life among people who don't.
   Beyond that class of question, widen everything.
2. **Widen the pool, shrink the roster.** Most people do the reverse: they filter heavily and then date a
   lot of people from the narrow result. Do the opposite — loosen the criteria, and keep **about three**
   people in active rotation at a time. To add someone, drop someone. As dating coach Logan Ury puts it: _"If
   expanding your settings means a bigger menu, then dating fewer people at a time means savoring each
   dish."_ Too many people in rotation makes the whole thing feel like a job and lowers the stakes for any
   one of them.
3. **Change the rooms you're in.** Not "where do I go to meet people to date" — _just be around people,
   period_, in contexts that repeat. This is module 8.

**This week.** Pick one filter you've been applying without ever having tested it — a job you find
unappealing, a personality type, a political shade, an age band, a height. Turn it off for a week and see
who's there.

## Module 6 — Using the apps without letting them use you

**What you probably believe.** That the app is a neutral pipe to a larger pool, and that being efficient
about it is the way to win.

**What the research shows.**

- **Choice overload is real and it works against you.** More options → less satisfaction with whoever you
  eventually pick, and more deferring the pick entirely. It's worse when options differ on many dimensions —
  which is exactly the case for people.
- **The rejection mindset.** You are most open-minded at the moment you start browsing and get steadily more
  rejecting as you go. Each reason you find to rule one person out gets added to the list you apply to
  everyone after. This is a property of sequential browsing, not of you.
- **Profiles predict excitement about profiles, and nothing else.** In a controlled experiment, participants
  saw a profile secretly written to match or mismatch their own stated ideals. On the profile, matching
  produced a solid boost in interest. After a **five-minute** in-person conversation, the two groups were
  **statistically identical**. Once you meet someone, their qualifications on paper go out the window.
- **The medium manufactures a gender gap.** At speed-dating events men say yes ~50% of the time and women
  ~35% — a small difference. On Tinder, men swipe right ~50% and women ~5% — a **tenfold** difference. Same
  species. The interface made the gap.
- **The economics are absurd.** The average user spends **~90 minutes a day** on dating apps, so that other
  people can look at them for **5–10 seconds** before finding one underwhelming detail and swiping left.
- **It costs something.** Tinder users report elevated appearance concerns and greater body shame than
  non-users, both men and women. Offline rejection is ambiguous and admits a hundred explanations; online,
  every silence reads as a verdict about you.
- And the apps aren't the root cause of the shift. In-person socialising collapsed first: teens in 2015
  socialised **an hour less per day** than earlier cohorts and went from 5 hours of parties a week in 2000 to
  2 in 2015 — over 40 fewer get-togethers a year. Meeting through friends fell off a cliff at exactly the
  moment gamified apps arrived. "You're not supposed to make a choice and log off; you're supposed to keep
  playing."

**What to do differently.**

> **The apps succeed when you spend your time swiping. You succeed when you use them only as a springboard
> to communing out in the real world.**

- **Widen the filters, shrink the roster** (module 5).
- **Get offline as soon as it's safe to.** People are meant to be experienced, not ranked. And when you get
  there, don't turn the date into an interview or a meeting squeezed between meetings. Do an _activity_:
  a class, skating, a tasting, karaoke, a match. "Don't schedule your dates on Tuesdays — when nothing in
  town is open — and then wonder why your dates are boring."
- **Cap the browsing, not the meeting.** The rejection mindset builds within a session. Short sessions with
  breaks beat one long scroll.
- **Make it collective again.** The most striking underused move in the book: when you meet someone from an
  app and there's no click — **introduce them to a friend**. Someone's "meh" is someone else's "mm-hmm."
  Eastwick's suggestion is a party where everyone brings a former date they didn't click with. Dating was a
  group activity for the entire history of the species until about 2013.

**This week.** Halve your app time and spend the other half somewhere with other humans in it. Then introduce
two people you know.

## Module 7 — First contact, and the third impression

**What you probably believe.** That your job on a date is to be impressive, and that you know after one
meeting whether there's something there.

**What the research shows.**

- **First impressions are unstable.** Tracking how people felt about the same prospective partners over
  months, the first impression predicts later feelings only modestly. Stability **jumps enormously at the
  second impression**, and improves again at the third. After that, diminishing returns. Your third
  impression is a better predictor of your own future feelings than anything else that's ever been measured.
- **Self-promotion backfires.** The self-protective instinct that comes with loneliness pushes people to
  perform — be funny, be charming, make them fall for you — and it produces _worse_ impressions.
- **Escalating self-disclosure works.** Aron's "fast friends" procedure — questions that escalate in
  intimacy over about an hour — reliably produces more closeness than small talk, and measurably raises the
  odds of a second date. People are up for more disclosure than we assume, even at a first meeting.
- **You're wrong about how it'll go.** People systematically **overestimate how awkward** a conversation
  with someone new will be and **underestimate how much they'll enjoy it**.
- **Preparation helps.** Pre-planning a couple of conversation topics measurably smooths a conversation.

**What to do differently.**

- **Decide after three impressions, not one.** An "impression" isn't a date — it's however long it takes for
  a coherent read to form, from minutes to a whole evening. Make the three _different_ — at most one of them
  a twenty-minute coffee.
- **Judge on four things**, and note that the fourth is the one people forget: physical attraction; how they
  make you feel; how much you enjoy being around them; and **how you feel about yourself when you're with
  them**. You want someone whose good qualities feel contagious — not someone who makes you feel small.
- **Be an audience, and don't require an audience.** Ask questions that keep the conversation going in the
  _same_ direction rather than pulling focus back to yourself.
- **Grant restarts.** Conversations are chaotic: one topic dies, the next one catches fire, and there's no
  way to know in advance. A dead-end topic is not a dead-end person. Modern fifteen-minute coffee dates
  don't allow enough restarts for the good topic to be found.
- **Spend five minutes preparing.** Read their profile properly and arrive with two real questions.

**Aron's escalating questions**, if you want them (don't jump to level 3 in the first five minutes):

_Level 1 — getting comfortable_

- For what in your life do you feel most grateful?
- Take four minutes and tell me your life story in as much detail as possible.

_Level 2 — getting real_

- If you could wake up tomorrow having gained any one quality or ability, what would it be?
- If a crystal ball could tell you the truth about yourself, your life, the future, or anything else, what
  would you want to know?
- Is there something you've dreamed of doing for a long time? Why haven't you done it?
- What does friendship mean to you?
- Complete this sentence: "I wish I had someone with whom I could share…"

_Level 3 — getting vulnerable_

- Tell me what you like about me; be honest, saying things you might not say to someone you just met.
- If you were to die this evening with no chance to communicate with anyone, what would you most regret not
  having told someone, and why haven't you told them yet?

**This week.** On your next first meeting, ask three follow-up questions before you tell your next story.

## Module 8 — Build a mixed-gender network, and treat the loneliness first

**What you probably believe.** That friendships with people you could be attracted to are a trap ("the
friendzone"), and that the way to find a partner is to go looking for one.

**What the research shows.**

- **Friends-first is the main road, not a detour.** Around **two-thirds** of relationships grow out of
  friendship. Among under-30s and LGBTQ people, **more than 80%** of relationships are friends-first. And
  even relationships that begin on an app follow a friendship-then-romance sequence most of the time. Meeting
  through friends is still what **65% of people say is the best way** to meet someone — against **1%** who
  say online is.
- **Gender-diverse networks predict finding a partner.** People with more gender-diverse friendship networks
  are more likely to find mutual attraction over time. For straight people, the number of **same-gender**
  friends is basically irrelevant to romantic prospects. Straight men with more female friends are also
  measurably less sexist and less likely to objectify women — "safer bets as boyfriend material."
- **Two hard caveats.** It takes about **a year**, on average, between becoming friends and turning
  romantic. And _deliberately_ trying to convert a friendship is a bad strategy: it's about **twice as
  likely** that both people develop feelings gradually than that one person successfully woos the other. So
  don't befriend someone with ulterior motives. If your attraction is so strong you can't genuinely be
  friends and it isn't mutual, step back.
- **The base rate is small and worth knowing.** Somewhere between **1% and 5%** of cross-gender
  acquaintances ever turn romantic. Forming a relationship is a rare event. Most friends stay friends. This
  is not a sign that something's wrong with you; it's the arithmetic.
- **Loneliness is a separate problem that masquerades as this one.** People routinely mistake wanting more
  people around for wanting a partner. Loneliness is subjective — it doesn't track how many friends you
  objectively have — and it runs on the **porcupine problem**: you want connection and simultaneously get
  vigilant for rejection, so you withdraw. What works at scale: **groups that meet regularly** and **events
  in the service of others** — repetition is the mechanism. What works individually: changing the
  self-protective thought (module 1). And: **in-person beats texting and video calls; anything mutual beats
  passively scrolling social media**, which makes lonely people lonelier.

**What to do differently.**

> **"If you don't have friends of your romantically preferred gender, you're doing it wrong. You should fix
> that."**

And the other line worth carrying around: **"Stop thinking about where you go to meet people to date. Just
be around people, period."**

- Join things that **recur**. A weekly league, a class, a choir, a volunteer shift, a running club. Recurrence
  is the active ingredient, not the activity.
- Treat the loneliness before the dating. It makes the search less frantic and more fun, and the two
  problems share most of the same solutions.
- Accept the timescale. If it happens through friendship, it will probably happen slowly and you won't
  notice it happening.
- Learn to end things gracefully, because if you date inside a network you'll have to. Sharing a social
  circle with an ex is, on average, neither good nor bad — carrying a torch is what hurts.

**This week.** Commit to one recurring thing for eight weeks. Not a one-off. Eight weeks.

## Module 9 — What actually makes a relationship good: safe haven and secure base

**What you probably believe.** That good relationships come from finding the right person, and that the
things that hold them together are chemistry and compatibility of personality.

**What the research shows.** Humans are one of the roughly **3–5%** of mammal species that pair-bond, and
the machinery we use is the infant-caregiver attachment system, repurposed. It has two functions:

- **Safe haven** — when something bad happens, you go to them for comfort, help, and validation.
- **Secure base** — when something good or daunting happens, they're the springboard: they encourage the
  attempt and they celebrate the win.

Both have **very large** effects on satisfaction, commitment **and passion** — they're bound up with the
feeling that a relationship is irreplaceable. They show up **before** a relationship formally exists, which
makes them the single best early signal that something is worth pursuing. And within a few months of dating,
"my partner" becomes most people's answer to _who do you turn to?_

**A self-check** (rate 1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree):

_Safe haven_

1. My partner is the person I'd want to go to, to help me feel better when something bad happens or I'm upset.
2. My partner is the first person I'd turn to when I have a problem.
3. My partner is my primary source of emotional support.

_Secure base_ 4. My partner is the person I'd want to go to, to be proud of me when something good happens or I want to celebrate. 5. If I achieved something good, my partner is the person I'd tell first. 6. My partner is a person whom I count on for advice.

Sum 1–3 for safe haven and 4–6 for secure base. **19–21 = high, 15–18 = medium, ≤14 = low.** These also
work for a close friend, and they work for someone you're just getting to know.

**And the other engine: communal norms.** There's a distinction between _exchange_ relationships (we do
things for each other and the ledger should balance) and _communal_ ones (we respond to each other's needs
without tracking who owes whom). People's preference for communal over exchange norms in romance is one of
the **largest effects in the entire field** — it dwarfs almost everything else measured about attraction and
relationships — and married couples report that theirs actually work that way. People who prioritise meeting
their partner's needs without expecting repayment are happier; **people who keep track of favours are less
happy.** As _The Bear_ puts it: _"Nobody's keeping track of shoes."_

Communal doesn't mean selfless. You still watch whether _your_ needs are being met. You just stop watching
the balance.

**What to do differently.**

_To be a safe haven:_

- **Create security.** Once you know you want to be with someone, say it, even though it's exposing. Nobody
  opens up if they think you'll bolt at the first sign of real emotion.
- **Practise listening.** Honest personal disclosures are rare, and when someone gives you one it means they
  trust you. Listening well is much harder when their problem doesn't seem like a big deal to you, or when
  you'd have handled it differently. Ask afterwards — _"did I give you the support you were looking for?"_ —
  and accept the answer.

_To be a secure base:_

- **Bask in their wins**, including the small daily ones. Ask for details. Show pride. Say in your own words
  why they deserve to be excited. This takes putting yourself on the back burner for a few minutes, which is
  exactly why people are bad at it.
- **Encourage the goals they actually have**, not the ones you'd have in their position.

_On scorekeeping:_ the urge to start counting who does more is a **warning sign**, not a character flaw. It
usually means a need has stopped being met, and it's usually triggered by external stress — stress both
reduces the time you spend together and poisons what's left. In a study of ~300 New Zealand parents during
Covid school closures, dissatisfaction spiked for whoever was doing more than half the housework _and_
getting less than half the leisure — regardless of how happy they'd been before. The couples who fared best
**renegotiated the split** rather than defending the old pattern.

**This week.** Score the six questions above about your partner — or about the closest person in your life.
Whichever score is lower, do one thing this week that raises it.

## Module 10 — Building the thing: tiny cultures and keeping the spark

**What the research shows.** Relationships that work are built out of three things, none of which existed
before the two people met:

1. **The story of us.** A vivid, coherent origin story as a couple is associated with satisfaction. It gets
   co-written, and you have real agency over what it says.
2. **Self-expansion.** Partners absorb each other's interests, values and attributes; the line between self
   and partner blurs. **Self–partner overlap predicts higher satisfaction, less infidelity, and lower
   breakup risk.** This is why the "I hate jazz" opening of _La La Land_ isn't a dealbreaker — the
   difference becomes the shared project.
3. **Pattern creation.** Rituals built from _that was good, let's do it again_. Private language, nicknames,
   silly customs, a protected mealtime, a standing walk. Researchers describe couples as **tiny two-person
   cultures**, and the customs aren't decoration — they're the thing that makes the relationship feel unique
   and irreplaceable. It's also why the reasons you work with one person don't transfer to the next: someone
   else's affectionate teasing routine would read as nagging in a different relationship.

**Patterns need renegotiating.** When circumstances change — a child, a job, a move — a pattern that was
carrying something important can vanish without anyone noticing what was lost. The book's example: a couple
whose whole relationship was built around cooking elaborate dinners together lost that time when meals
started revolving around their kid, and a distance crept in before either of them understood why. _"To fix a
broken pattern, couples must identify what is implicit, make it explicit, and be willing to rearrange the
pieces."_

**On passion fading.** It does fade, and the usual explanation — that intimacy kills desire — is probably
wrong. What fades is **mystery and curiosity**, which come free at the start and then get crowded out by
responsibilities and exhaustion. Two remedies, both evidenced: **stay separate people** with independent
goals and growth outside the relationship, so there's something new to bring back; and **spend time together
in ways that subvert the routine** — get away from work and the ordinary and you'll find the desire is still
there.

**One more thing worth knowing about being in a relationship:** you're not objective, and that's largely
functional. Couples systematically see each other in the best light, reframe faults ("he can be withdrawn,
_but_ he shows me he cares in so many other ways" — the "yes, but" pattern predicts the longest-lasting
relationships), become _more_ certain their relationship is special when told it'll probably fail, and
literally perceive attractive alternatives as less attractive than single people do. A stray fantasy about
someone else tends to rebound into more desire for your actual partner. Historically, only about **1% of
children** were fathered by someone other than the mother's partner — compared with ~10% in pair-bonding
birds and ~40% in other pair-bonding mammals. Whatever else humans are, we are unusually good at staying
bonded.

**This week.** Name one ritual you've lost and one you've never had. Reinstate the first; try the second.

## Module 11 — Staying, leaving, and the one real red flag

**What the research shows.** The biases in module 10 cut both ways. They keep good relationships alive
through hard patches, and they keep bad ones alive past the point they should have ended. Eastwick is unusually
blunt that the science has no answer here: _"there is no manual for determining when the biases set people
down a good or bad path,"_ and for a relationship that's currently fine, it's nearly impossible to forecast a
turn.

What is known:

- **You're not fixed.** If you're unhappy in one relationship, your odds in the next are about **60/40**
  unhappy — better than a coin flip, nowhere near destiny. Two of your own exes comparing notes about what
  you're like to be with would agree at roughly chance.
- **Therapy works, generally.** Psychotherapy _causes_ people to be happier in relationships across the
  board, partnered or not, and regardless of who they're with.
- **Take longer between relationships.** A gap of a couple of years rather than a few months predicts being
  considerably happier in the next one.
- **Attachment style is a tendency, not a fate.** The link between childhood experience and adult attachment
  style is fairly small; responsive partners later in life make people durably more secure. Even for
  polyamorous people, being avoidant with one partner only predicts being avoidant with a second about 58%
  of the time.
- **Generic relationship-skills programmes don't work.** Large-scale communication and conflict seminars have
  been disappointments — not because people can't understand "don't stonewall," but because those skills have
  to be built inside a particular relationship's own logic. Couples therapy that _does_ work involves a third
  party who learns your specific patterns and origin story and helps you make the implicit rules explicit.
- **The one validated hard red flag is proactive aggression** — a systematic, sustained, cold strategy to
  intimidate, threaten, frighten, humiliate, control, or destroy your belongings. This is distinct from
  _reactive_ hot-headedness (flying off the handle when insulted), which therapy can address. Proactive
  aggression is a hard no. Leave, even when you know the contrition is coming. These people should not be in
  relationships.

**What to do differently.** Ask green-flag questions rather than hunting red flags: no partner checks every
box, so the useful question is whether you **want** to build something around the parts that work and
compartmentalise the parts that don't. And if you're carrying baggage: working on yourself does help — but
it also helps for a second, less obvious reason. When people work on themselves they take classes, join
leagues, volunteer, go back to church, spend time at the dog park. Their **networks expand as a side
effect**, and that's where the relationships come from.

## The whole thing on one page

1. **You don't have a number.** People who actually know you agree about your desirability 53% of the time.
2. **Your advantage or disadvantage in looks is maximal in photos and first meetings, and decays from
   there.** Choose contexts with repeated exposure.
3. **Men and women want nearly the same things.** The differences are in what people _say_, not what they
   respond to. The real one is women's wariness of casual sex with strangers — mostly a safety response.
4. **Nobody is a hookup type or a marriage type.** 86% of the time the two judgments coincide anyway.
5. **Compatibility can't be predicted.** Not by similarity, not by your must-haves, not by love languages,
   not by an algorithm — the measured ceiling is 0–4%.
6. **Compatibility is constructed** — from repeated interaction, a shared story, absorbed interests, and
   private rituals. _That was good, let's do it again._
7. **Curate the pool, not the picks.** It's the only real lever. Filter on the structural; widen everything
   else; keep about three people in rotation.
8. **Decide after the third impression**, not the first. And count how you feel about _yourself_ when
   you're with them.
9. **Be an audience, don't require one.** Disclose more than feels natural, escalate slowly, grant restarts.
10. **Build a mixed-gender network and treat the loneliness first.** >80% of relationships among under-30s
    are friends-first. It takes about a year. Don't do it with ulterior motives.
11. **Apps are a springboard, not a venue.** Get offline fast, do activities, and introduce your rejects to
    your friends.
12. **Once you're in it:** be a safe haven and a secure base, drop the ledger, build rituals, renegotiate
    them when life changes, and leave for proactive aggression.

## What this module can't tell you

Stated because the honest version is more useful than a confident one.

- **It cannot tell you who to pick.** That's the central finding, not a gap in the writing.
- **It cannot tell you when to leave** a relationship that isn't abusive but isn't right. No one can, yet.
- **The revealed-preference research on gay men and lesbian women is thin** — Eastwick calls it an
  unfortunate gap in the literature. Most of the attraction findings above come from mixed-gender studies,
  even though the relationship-maintenance findings hold across orientations.
- **"Change your scenery" assumes you can.** Moving cities, joining paid classes and restructuring your week
  are not equally available to everyone, and the book says so.
- **These are averages.** A medium effect means you'd guess right 65% of the time. It does not mean it will
  be true of you.
